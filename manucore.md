**Abstract**

**Background:** In the summer of 2026, the UK and most other European countries experienced a series of heatwaves with unprecedented intensity and frequency. Here we extended an established framework for small-area health impact forecasting to characterise temporal, demographic, and geographical patterns of heat-related excess mortality across England and Wales from 1 May to 30 September 2026.

**Methods:** Daily mean temperature estimates from ERA5-Land and ERA5 reanalyses were linked to age-specific temperature–mortality functions for 34,753 Lower Layer Super Output Areas and combined with population data and regional mortality rates.

**Results:** The model estimated 5,827 excess deaths (95% empirical confidence interval [eCI]: 4,349-6,897) across the study period, equivalent to 97.6 per million population (72.8–115.5). People aged 75 years or older accounted for 4,408 deaths (3,367-5,334), representing 75.7% of the total. Five identified heatwave periods contributed an estimated 5,325 deaths, or 91.4% of the seasonal total. Burden was greatest during the periods in 18–28 June (1,808 deaths; 1,306–2,140) and 6–17 July (1,468; 1,072–1,750). Daily modelled excess deaths peaked on 26 June (409 deaths; 306–475) and 13 August (405; 276–479). Regional rates ranged from 41.7 deaths per million (5.4–70.9) in the Northeast to 133.9 (101.8–156.0) in the West Midlands, with similarly high rates in the Southeast of England (133.0; 92.2–166.3) and London (132.4; 102.1–157.0).

**Conclusion:** In summary, we found heat-related mortality to be strongly episodic, concentrated among older adults, and unevenly distributed across small areas during the UK’s record-breaking summer.

**Keywords:** Heatwaves; excess mortality; temperature; older adults; spatial analysis; England and Wales

**What this study adds**

Previous rapid assessments, published in non-peer-reviewed reports, quantified mortality during the May and June 2026 heatwaves. This study extends the evidence across the complete May–September season and provides daily, age-specific, regional, and LSOA-level estimates. The analysis provides an astonishing figure of 5,827 excess deaths occurring in England and Wales across the summer. We found that 91.4% of estimated mortality occurred within five identified heatwave periods and 75.7% among people aged 75 years or older, with substantial geographical variation. The analysis shows how the burden accumulated during the record-breaking summer and identifies when, where, and among whom impacts were greatest.

We estimated daily and cumulative modelled heat-related excess mortality in England and Wales from 1 May to 30 September 2026, and described how the burden varied over time, by age group and region, and across LSOAs, before complete mortality registrations for 2026 were available. We applied an established small-area framework to reanalysis temperature estimates, alongside age- and location-specific temperature–mortality functions, population data, and baseline mortality.3,10

**Methods**

The assessment covered 34,753 Lower Layer Super Output Areas (LSOAs; 2011 boundaries) in England and Wales from 1 May to 30 September 2026. Daily mean and maximum 2-m temperatures, adjusted to local time, were retrieved for each LSOA centroid through the Open-Meteo Historical Weather API, using the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) ERA5-Land reanalysis where available, and ERA5 for coastal LSOAs where ERA5-Land data were unavailable.11-13 Daily maximum temperature was summarised descriptively to characterise heat exposure; daily mean temperature was used as the exposure in the mortality model. We combined temperature data with 2020 LSOA-level population data and 2024 regional all-cause mortality rates by age group.14,15 Analyses were conducted for ages 0–64, 65–74, 75–84, and 85 years or older.

We applied previously estimated age- and LSOA-specific temperature–mortality functions for England and Wales within an established small-area health impact framework.3,10 The functions were derived from mortality and temperature data for 2000–2019 using distributed lag non-linear models over lags of 0–21 days, multivariate meta-regression, and small-area prediction.3 For each LSOA and age group, the cumulative exposure–response function was represented by a quadratic B-spline with knots at the LSOA-specific 10th, 75th, and 90th percentiles of the historical temperature distribution. The minimum mortality temperature was identified within the 1st–99th percentile range and used as the reference for heat-excess mortality.

Annual expected deaths were calculated from 2024 regional mortality rates and 2020 LSOA populations, then allocated across days using region- and age-specific seasonal weights derived from Office for National Statistics (ONS) daily death counts for 2010–2019.16 Specifically, normalised 365-day seasonal weights were estimated separately by region and age group using quasi-Poisson models with a periodic B-spline for day of year and a natural spline for calendar time.17 Heat-attributable fractions derived from cumulative relative risks were then applied to estimate daily excess deaths. Estimates were indexed to the exposure day and incorporated effects over lags of 0–21 days.3 We aggregated estimates by date, age group, region, LSOA, and analytical period. Cumulative burdens were expressed as modelled excess deaths and excess deaths per million population.

For event-specific analyses, we defined five heatwave periods informed by UKHSA heat-health alerts and corresponding peaks in the LSOA-averaged temperature series: 22–28 May, 18–28 June, 6–17 July, 29 July–5 August, and 11–15 August 2026.7,18 The same windows were applied across all LSOAs. 95% empirical confidence intervals (eCIs) were derived from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of excess-death estimates across 500 Monte Carlo samples drawn from the multivariate-normal distribution of the model coefficients.19 We descriptively compared our estimates with a rapid model-based attribution assessment and interim heat mortality monitoring estimates from the UKHSA,7,8 matching the dates and geographic coverage reported in each source.

**Results**

Across England and Wales, estimated LSOA-level daily maximum temperature reached 36.9°C on 25 June 2026. Daily maximum temperature exceeded 30°C in at least one LSOA on 32 days. The exceedance was most widespread on 13 August, when 30,127 (86.7%) of the 34,753 LSOAs exceeded 30°C (Figure S1).

From 1 May to 30 September 2026, the model estimated 5,827 heat-related excess deaths (95% eCI, 4,349 to 6,897) in England and Wales, corresponding to 97.6 deaths per million population (72.8 to 115.5). The burden increased sharply with age. People aged 75 years or older, who comprised 8.7% of the study population, accounted for 4,408 deaths (3,367 to 5,334), or 75.7% of the total. People aged 85 years or older accounted for 3,133 deaths (2,338 to 3,828), more than half of the all-age estimate. The cumulative burden ranged from 18.3 deaths per million (6.0 to 28.6) among people aged 0–64 years to 2,100.2 per million (1,567.3 to 2,566.3) among those aged 85 years or older (Table 1, Table S2).

The five identified heatwave periods accounted for 5,325 deaths, or 91.4% of the study-period estimate. The largest burden occurred during 18–28 June, with 1,808 deaths (1,306 to 2,140), followed by 6–17 July, with 1,468 deaths (1,072 to 1,750). Daily total estimates peaked on 26 June at 409 deaths (306 to 475) and on 13 August at 405 deaths (276 to 479) (Figure 1).

The largest cumulative regional counts occurred in the South East, with 1,226 deaths (850 to 1,533), and London, with 1,192 deaths (919 to 1,413). Burdens per million population were highest in the West Midlands (133.9; 101.8 to 156.0), South East (133.0; 92.2 to 166.3), and London (132.4; 102.1 to 157.0), and lowest in the North East (41.7; 5.4 to 70.9). Wales had an estimated 241 excess deaths (97 to 341), equivalent to 76.0 per million population (30.7 to 107.7) (Table 1). LSOA-level maps showed strong spatial variation within regions and differences in the geographical pattern between heat episodes (Figure 2). When matched by date and geographical coverage, our estimates were generally consistent with previously published assessments, although lower in four of five comparisons (Table S1).

**Discussion**

Across the extended 2026 heat season, the modelled heat-related mortality burden was concentrated in a few episodes, disproportionately among older adults, and unevenly distributed geographically. The five identified heatwave periods accounted for 91.4% of the study-period point estimate, although smaller late-season peaks occurred outside them. People aged 75 years or older accounted for 75.7% of estimated deaths, despite representing 8.7% of the population. We found strong geographical differences: regional counts were highest in the South East and London, while burden per million was highest in the West Midlands, followed closely by the South East and London, and lowest in the North East. For context, the ONS’s separate 1988–2025 analysis estimated the largest number of deaths associated with the hottest 2.5% days in London.20

This study extends previous work focused on a short event in 2022 to a 153-day assessment of the 2026 heat season. Applying a consistent framework throughout allowed the five analytical heat episodes and intervening periods to be evaluated on the same basis and showed that only 8.6% of the modelled burden accumulated outside the selected periods. The age gradient and fine-scale spatial variation accord with the historical small-area risk analysis and the framework’s application to the July 2022 heatwave.3,10 In matched comparisons, our estimates were consistent, although slightly lower, than previous assessments,7,8 likely in part because of differences in data and methods.

Our findings also complement the 2026 European assessment, which estimated 4,354 heat-related deaths (3,491 to 5,227) in the included UK urban areas during June–August.9 Direct comparison with our estimate is limited because that assessment focused on urban adults aged 20 years or older and climate attribution, whereas ours covered all ages and the full population of England and Wales from May to September. Our analysis therefore adds daily, age-, event- and small-area-specific detail. The related 2000–2025 analysis of the same European cities showed that the contribution of anthropogenic warming to heat-related mortality increased over time, providing longer-term context for the exceptionally high burden estimated in 2026.21

The findings support age-targeted and local preparedness. Counts describe absolute mortality burden, whereas rates indicate burden relative to population size. LSOA-level estimates may complement regional alerts by showing small-area variation relevant to local risk assessment,10,22 but service-level planning would require aggregation to the relevant health-service geographies. Smaller peaks outside the selected periods support surveillance and preparedness throughout the warm season beyond national or regional heat-health alerts. This accords with England’s Adverse Weather and Health Plan, which specifies year-round planning and permits alerts outside the core summer season.23 Our retrospective findings also motivate evaluation of impact-based heat-health forecasts to guide age- and area-targeted outreach and service readiness alongside existing alerts.

Some limitations must be acknowledged. First, these are modelled estimates of excess deaths associated with ERA5-Land/ERA5 reanalysis-based temperatures for 2026 and historical mortality data, not registered counts of heat-related deaths. In addition, risk functions estimated for 2000–2019 may not fully represent current susceptibility due to population adaptation.24 Similarly, seasonal mortality patterns derived from daily death counts for 2010–2019, together with population and mortality inputs from 2020 and 2024 may not fully represent the current scenario. Reanalysis temperatures assigned at LSOA centroids may not capture within-area variation, particularly in larger or geographically heterogeneous LSOAs. The five episodes are analytical rather than official periods, and their contribution depends on the selected boundaries. Finally, empirical confidence intervals include risk-function coefficient uncertainty, but not uncertainty in exposure, population, baseline mortality, episode selection, or model transportability. Within these constraints, the analysis provides a timely multiscale assessment of the extended 2026 heat season.

**Conclusion**

Our model estimated a high excess mortality due to heat during summer 2026 in England and Wales. The impact was strongly episodic, concentrated among older adults and unevenly distributed geographically across the two countries. These findings support age-targeted and locally tailored preparedness and motivate evaluation of impact-based heat-health forecasts alongside existing alerts.
